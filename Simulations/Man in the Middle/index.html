Understanding the Attack

Step 1: The Secure Baseline (Normal Routing)

🎯 The Simple Story
Imagine an armored truck driving directly from your house to the bank using a locked safe. The route is well-known and trusted—street signs point the right way, and no one interferes with the journey. Your sensitive documents travel securely inside the locked safe, protected from prying eyes.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 2 Layer 3 Layer 7
In normal network operations, your device uses Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) to map IP addresses to MAC addresses at Layer 2 (Data Link Layer). When you visit https://bank.com, your browser initiates a TLS handshake at Layer 7 (Application Layer), establishing an encrypted channel. Traffic flows directly from Victim → Gateway → Internet with proper Layer 3 (Network Layer) IP routing. The encryption ensures confidentiality and integrity via cryptographic protocols like AES-256 and SHA-256.
💡 Key Concept: ARP is a stateless protocol—it trusts any ARP reply without verification, which is the fundamental vulnerability exploited in MitM attacks.

Step 2: The Interception (ARP Poisoning)

🎯 The Simple Story
You secretly replace all the street signs in the neighborhood. You tell the house "I am the bank" and tell the bank "I am the house." Now the armored truck thinks it should drive through your warehouse before reaching its destination. Everyone believes your fake GPS updates because they never verify the source.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 2
The attacker broadcasts forged ARP Reply packets (unsolicited, known as "Gratuitous ARP"). These packets map the attacker's MAC address to the Gateway's IP address (192.168.1.1) on the victim's machine, and vice versa. This is ARP Cache Poisoning or ARP Spoofing. Since ARP is stateless and lacks authentication, the victim updates its ARP cache with the malicious entry. All traffic destined for the gateway now flows through the attacker's machine at Layer 2.
⚠️ Vulnerability: ARP has no authentication mechanism. Any device can claim any IP address belongs to its MAC address.

Step 3: The Eavesdrop (Packet Sniffing)

🎯 The Simple Story
Now the locked safes roll through your warehouse. You can't see inside them yet (they're still encrypted), but you know exactly who is talking to whom, when they communicate, and how much data they're exchanging. You're a silent observer with a perfect vantage point.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 2 Layer 3
The attacker enables IP forwarding (echo 1 > /proc/sys/net/ipv4/ip_forward on Linux) to prevent network disruption. Using packet capture tools like Wireshark, tcpdump, or Bettercap, the attacker sniffs all traffic passing through their network interface. At this stage, HTTPS traffic remains encrypted, but metadata (IPs, ports, packet sizes, timing) is visible. This is passive reconnaissance before active exploitation.
🔍 What's Visible: Source/destination IPs, TCP/UDP ports, packet sizes, timing patterns, DNS queries (if unencrypted), and unencrypted protocols (HTTP, FTP, Telnet).

Step 4: The Downgrade (SSL Stripping)

🎯 The Simple Story
When the victim tries to send a locked safe, you intercept it. You tell the victim "Oh, we don't need the safe—just use a regular paper envelope." Meanwhile, you use a locked safe to talk to the real bank. The victim thinks they're being secure, but you've downgraded their protection to plaintext.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 7
SSL Stripping (or SSL Downgrade Attack) exploits the initial HTTP→HTTPS redirect. When the victim requests http://bank.com, the server responds with a 301/302 redirect to https://bank.com. The attacker intercepts this, strips the redirect, and serves the victim a plaintext HTTP version while maintaining HTTPS to the real server. This is a transparent proxy attack. Tools like SSLstrip or Bettercap's https module automate this.
🚨 Critical: Without HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security), browsers accept the downgraded connection. HSTS forces HTTPS-only connections, killing this attack.

Step 5: The Capture (Credential Harvesting)

🎯 The Simple Story
The victim writes their secret code (username and password) on the unsealed paper envelope and sends it. As it passes through your hands, you read it, copy the secret code, and then forward it along to the bank. The victim never knows you've stolen their credentials.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 7
The victim submits credentials via an HTTP POST request over the downgraded plaintext connection. The attacker's packet sniffer captures the full HTTP request body, extracting form parameters (username, password), session cookies, and any other sensitive data. This is credential harvesting or session hijacking. Regex patterns or tools like Bettercap's proxy module can automatically extract and log credentials in real-time.
🎯 Impact: Full account compromise, session hijacking, lateral movement, and potential data exfiltration. All without triggering security alerts.

Step 6: Enterprise Defense (HSTS & DAI)

🎯 The Simple Story
The bank issues strict rules: "Armored trucks must NEVER accept paper envelopes—only locked safes." Additionally, the city upgrades all street signs with anti-tamper technology that verifies authenticity. Now your fake GPS updates are rejected, and the armored truck refuses to downgrade its security.
⚙️ The Industry Mechanics Layer 2 Layer 7
HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security): A Layer 7 defense where servers send the Strict-Transport-Security header, instructing browsers to only use HTTPS for a specified duration (max-age). This prevents SSL stripping by enforcing encrypted connections.

Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI): A Layer 2 switch security feature that validates ARP packets against a trusted database (built via DHCP Snooping). Forged ARP replies are dropped, blocking ARP poisoning.

802.1X Authentication: Port-based network access control requiring devices to authenticate before joining the network, preventing unauthorized attackers from connecting.
✅ Defense in Depth: Combine HSTS, DAI, 802.1X, network segmentation, and encrypted DNS (DNS-over-HTTPS/TLS) for comprehensive protection against MitM attacks.
